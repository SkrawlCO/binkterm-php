# Crossroads — Product Vision

## The idea

**Crossroads is a modern gaming community built around a living BBS.**

It combines the accessibility and social awareness people expect from modern
online communities with the human scale, independence, personality, and
sense of place that made BBS communities special.

Crossroads is not an attempt to recreate the Internet of the 1990s.

It asks a different question:

> **What would a BBS community look like if its evolution had never stopped?**

The answer should feel familiar to someone who grew up calling BBSes—and
completely approachable to someone who has never heard a modem handshake.

No nostalgia is required.

## Why Crossroads exists

Modern community platforms are extraordinarily capable, but capability has
often come with complexity, centralization, noise, and systems designed
around capturing attention.

Communities become feeds. Conversations become streams. Discovery becomes
recommendation algorithms. Identity becomes a profile owned by somebody
else's platform.

There is another model.

A community can be a **place**.

A place with recognizable people. Places to gather. Things to play.
Conversations that don't disappear beneath thousands of newer messages.
A little history. A little weirdness. A SysOp who actually lives there.

BBSes understood that remarkably well.

Crossroads takes that human model seriously while refusing to pretend
technology stopped advancing thirty years ago.

## The two parents

Crossroads inherits from **modern gaming communities**:

- immediate web access
- approachable identity
- presence and "who's here"
- profiles and social context
- activity and discovery
- persistent games and worlds
- easy ways to enter and return
- rich presentation where it helps
- low-friction onboarding

Crossroads inherits from **BBS culture**:

- human-scale communities
- SysOp stewardship
- local identity and character
- asynchronous conversation
- doors and shared games
- FTN and connections between independent systems
- terminal access
- community ownership
- independence from giant platforms
- experimentation and weirdness
- the feeling that you are visiting a place

Neither parent wins.

**Crossroads exists where they meet.**

## The BBS is the heart

The BBS is not a retro feature hidden in a corner of Crossroads.

It is its technological and cultural heart.

Web, terminal, games, worlds, conversations, presence, activity, and future
surfaces should increasingly feel like different ways of participating in
the **same community**.

That does not mean every surface must contain every feature.

The web can lead where modern presentation creates new possibilities. The
terminal can remain intentionally different where its constraints are part
of its character.

But neither should become a second-class experience.

A person entering through a browser and a person entering through SyncTERM
should recognize that they have arrived at the same place.

## Crossroads is not a BBS skin

Putting a modern CSS framework on a BBS does not create Crossroads.

Neither does adding ANSI graphics to a modern community platform.

The connection has to be deeper.

Identity should carry meaning across experiences.

Presence should tell us where our people are.

Games and worlds should feel like places inside the community rather than
unrelated applications launched from a menu.

Conversation should connect people around those experiences.

Activity should help people understand what is happening without becoming
an attention-maximizing feed.

The technology underneath Crossroads can be complicated.

The community should not have to feel complicated because of it.

> **Complex infrastructure should enable a simpler community, not create a
> more complicated one.**

## A place, not a feed

Crossroads should optimize for **belonging, not engagement**.

We are not trying to maximize minutes spent, clicks generated,
notifications opened, or content consumed.

Success looks different:

You recognize names.

You notice someone is playing something interesting.

You join them.

You leave a message and somebody discovers it later.

You return to a world you were exploring yesterday.

You wander somewhere unexpected.

You teach somebody what a door game is.

Someone who has never touched a terminal becomes curious enough to try.

A regular disappears for six months and can come back without feeling like
they missed an infinite scroll of history.

The community remembers them.

## Modern without the machinery

Crossroads should freely adopt good ideas from modern platforms.

But modernity does not require importing all of their incentives.

Crossroads should be skeptical of:

- algorithmic engagement feeds
- manipulative notifications
- meaningless streaks
- artificial urgency
- popularity contests disguised as discovery
- gamification without community purpose
- metrics whose primary purpose is making people use the product more
- forcing every form of communication into one endless real-time stream
- complexity added merely because modern platforms have trained us to
  expect it

Features earn their place by strengthening the community.

## Nostalgia is welcome, but never required

BBS veterans should find things that make them grin.

ANSI.

Door games.

FTN addresses.

Netmail.

Echomail.

The terminal cursor blinking back at them.

Those things are part of the culture and should not be embarrassed away.

But understanding them cannot be an admission requirement.

A new member should be able to arrive through the web, meet people, discover
a game, enter a persistent world, participate in a conversation, and become
part of L33TEST without knowing what `DOOR32.SYS` means.

Then someday they might notice:

**Connect via Terminal**

…and wonder what happens if they click it.

That curiosity is how BBS culture reaches another generation.

## Experiences are the architecture. Crossroads is the place.

Internally, BinkTermPHP's generic **Experience** architecture provides the
machinery for discovering, presenting, entering, resuming, and understanding
games and worlds across different backends and surfaces.

Those generic boundaries matter and should remain generic.

**Crossroads is the human expression of that machinery at L33TEST.**

People should not need to understand backend types, session tables, adapters,
or launch authorities.

They should understand:

Who's here?

What's happening?

Where can I go?

What can I play?

Where was I?

What are my friends doing?

What's new since I was here?

That's Crossroads.

## Small is not failure

Crossroads does not need thousands of users to justify its existence.

Human-scale is part of the proposition.

A quiet room should feel peaceful rather than defective.

Three people doing interesting things together can matter more than three
thousand anonymous users scrolling past one another.

We should never manufacture activity to make the community appear larger
than it is.

If L33TEST is young, say so.

If it's quiet tonight, that's okay.

If someone arrives early enough to help shape the place, **that can be an
invitation rather than an embarrassment.**

Growth matters only insofar as it produces a healthier community.

## Independence matters

Crossroads can connect to Discord, forums, games, networks, and other
services without surrendering its identity to them.

External platforms can be useful doors into the community.

They should not become the foundation on which the community's continued
existence depends.

Where practical, L33TEST should own its identity, history, relationships,
and experiences.

Connection is good.

Dependency deserves scrutiny.

## Games change. People remain.

L33TEST is, and will remain, a community that plays modern games—out in
the world, on platforms and in places L33TEST does not control.

Those games, and the platforms and destinations around them, will change.
That churn is normal; it is not a problem to solve.

Crossroads does not try to replace those places, pull that activity
inside L33TEST, or pretend every game must run on L33TEST infrastructure.

What Crossroads keeps is the connection back—to L33TEST's people, its
identity, its conversation, its activity, its worlds, and the community
they share.

External destinations can come and go. The way home should not.

> **Games change. People remain. Crossroads bring them home.**

That is why the front door holds two different things. **What Lives
Here** is the relatively durable catalog of experiences L33TEST hosts or
integrates. **What We're Playing** is changing community state—what the
people here are into right now.

This is a product and design principle, not an instruction to build a
Crossroads page for every game today.

## The SysOp still matters

Crossroads retains something increasingly uncommon on the modern Internet:

**someone is responsible for the place.**

The SysOp is not merely a server administrator.

The role includes stewardship—setting the character of the community,
maintaining its spaces, connecting it with other systems, welcoming people,
experimenting, and protecting what makes the place worth visiting.

Technology should strengthen that stewardship rather than replace it with
opaque platform policy or algorithms.

## What Crossroads should feel like

Not enormous.

**Alive.**

Not frictionless at every cost.

**Welcoming.**

Not addictive.

**Worth returning to.**

Not polished into anonymity.

**Distinctive.**

Not nostalgic for its own sake.

**Rooted.**

Not technologically primitive.

**Understandable.**

Not a collection of integrations.

**A place.**

## The feature test

Before adding something to Crossroads, ask:

> **Does this strengthen the connection between the BBS, its people, and its
> experiences?**

Then ask:

> **Does it make the community more understandable or more complicated?**

And finally:

> **Are we building this because our community needs it, or because modern
> platforms taught us that software like this is supposed to have it?**

A feature that fails those tests should have an unusually good reason to
exist.

## The promise

Someone should be able to discover L33TEST without knowing what a BBS is.

They should be able to participate without learning thirty years of
terminology.

And if they stay long enough, they should eventually realize something:

They didn't join another platform.

**They found a little corner of the Internet that belongs to the people who
are there.**

That's Crossroads.

## Architectural ownership

The product vision does not change the ownership boundaries of the software.

**BinkTermPHP** is the generic BBS platform. Improvements that make sense
for BBS SysOps generally should remain upstream-friendly and reusable.

**Experience** is the generic architecture inside BinkTermPHP for discovery,
presentation, state, participation, launch, presence, activity, and related
cross-backend concepts. Keep these internals generic.

**Crossroads** is L33TEST's human-facing product expression of those generic
capabilities.

**L33TEST** is the actual community and the place in which Crossroads,
BinkTermPHP, telnet, FTN, games, worlds, forums, Discord, and future
connections live.

Before implementing a Crossroads idea, ask whether the underlying capability
should improve BinkTermPHP for everyone. If yes, build the generic primitive
upstream-friendly and let Crossroads consume it. If not, keep the
L33TEST-specific expression local.

Do not push L33TEST branding or product assumptions into generic Experience
internals.
